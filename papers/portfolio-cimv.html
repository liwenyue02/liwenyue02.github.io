Abstract

Invest a little now, inject the rest later.

We study portfolio optimization where investors allocate part of their capital across a large asset pool first, then inject the remaining capital gradually over subsequent periods. The proposed multi-period mean–variance model builds a sparse long-term strategy in this setting: a fused-Lasso formulation with two ℓ1 penalties lowers both holding and trading costs, and a two-block alternating direction method of multipliers solves the resulting non-smooth, multi-variable program with a full convergence analysis. Experiments on two real datasets confirm the practical benefit.

Approach

An overview of CIMV.

Most multi-period models assume a single lump-sum investment. That overlooks dollar-cost averaging and value averaging, where capital arrives in instalments — and it ignores the two costs that dominate a long-horizon book: holding and trading.

Holding cost

Every name you keep must be monitored, reported, and financed. Penalizing ‖wj‖1 pushes the optimizer toward a sparse portfolio, so capital concentrates in fewer, better-understood positions.

Trading cost

Frequent rebalancing churns the book. Penalizing ‖wj+1 − wj‖1 keeps weights stable from one period to the next, which lowers turnover and preserves allocation intent.

Let w stack the weights of all m periods and p the injected amounts. The compact form of the capital-injection mean–variance (CIMV) model is a quadratic objective with two ℓ1 terms and linear wealth dynamics.

minw, p   ½ w⊤Σw + λ1‖w‖1 + λ2‖Fw‖1
s.t.   Ew − p = y1,   H1p = y2,   p ≥ 0,   Gw − H2p ≥ wmin

Eq. (7) in the paper. Σ is the return covariance matrix; F is the first-difference matrix that measures weight changes between consecutive periods; E, G encode wealth evolution; H1, H2 encode the injection budget and the minimum-wealth floor.

  • Injection budget. Capital is injected over the first t periods and discounted at the risk-free rate; the remainder waits in the risk-free asset.
  • Non-negativity. p ≥ 0 — injections are one-directional, so the model does not allow withdrawing capital mid-horizon.
  • Wealth floor. Expected wealth in every period must beat the naive 1/n benchmark.

How it works

A two-block ADMM that provably converges.

CIMV has five separable blocks. A direct multi-block ADMM is not guaranteed to converge, so we use the prediction–correction scheme of He et al.: a Gauss–Seidel prediction step followed by a relaxation correction with parameter ν ∈ (0, 1).

Hover any tile to zoom it and expand the detail — the three update rules live inside one short loop.

Step 1 · ADMM iterate
Predict, block by block
w̃k = D1λk
D1 = (Σ + βA1⊤A1)−1A1⊤
The w̃-update is one linear solve with a matrix that is fixed across iterations, so we precompute its inverse by Cholesky. Every other block — p̃, ã, b̃, c̃ — uses a closed-form proximal map.
Step 2 · Soft-threshold
Both ℓ1 penalties share one proximal operator
ûi = sign(vi) · max{0, |vi| − λ/μ}
Every non-smooth piece — the holding penalty λ1‖w‖1 and the trading penalty λ2‖Fw‖1 — reduces to the same componentwise soft-threshold, cheap and exact.
Step 3 · Relax + convergence
Correct and relax to restore contraction
ξk+1 = ξk − N(ξk − ξ̃k)
Predicted values are relaxed by ν ∈ (0, 1) before the next iterate. This is what makes the direct multi-block ADMM — which does not converge on its own — recover the contraction property; the merit function ‖ξk − ξ*‖H decreases monotonically.

Per iteration cost: one matrix–vector product for w, a soft-threshold for each ℓ1 block, and one trivial update for the dual variable λ — well within reach for 83 assets × 6 periods.

Proposition A.1

Convergence. Let {vk} be the sequence generated by the algorithm. With H and Q positive definite, the merit function ‖ξk − ξ*‖H decreases monotonically and ‖ξk − ξ̃k‖Q → 0, so the sequence converges to an optimal solution point v*.

Takeaways

Four things to remember.

  1. Capital injections cut risk, not just cost

    On FF49 the variance-reduction ratio reaches 8.06 against 5.92 for the classic model; on FTSE 100 it never falls below 3.45 across the τ sweep.

  2. Two ℓ1 terms make the book sparse and stable

    Holding density stays between 18% and 54% and turnover drops relative to the naive 1/n book, so both costs are controlled by one tunable pair (τ1, τ2).

  3. The solver is provably convergent

    A two-block prediction–correction ADMM with Cholesky precomputation converges to an optimal solution; the proof shows the merit function decreases monotonically.

  4. Performance holds across targets

    IR stays above 0.49 on FTSE 100 and peaks at 0.8913 on FF49, so the risk reduction does not come at the expense of risk-adjusted return.
